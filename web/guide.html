The Analytics guide

Every tab of the Analytics panel, explained with a picture of it. Each picture is a drawn copy of part of the tab; numbered markers in the picture match the numbered points beside it.

The pictures use example numbers, marked as examples. Your own panel shows the real figures. Everything is measured from BCT's live feeds, mostly from the buses' GPS.

The controls at the top

These sit above every tab and apply to all of them.

  1. 1
    Period. Today, the last 7 days, or the last 30. Every tab uses it. Longer periods give steadier numbers.
  2. 2
    Route. Show all routes, or narrow every tab to one. Clicking a route badge or a route's row anywhere in the panel sets this too.
  3. 3
    Tabs. One per kind of analysis. Each has its own chapter below.
  4. 4
    CSV. Downloads that tab's numbers as a spreadsheet file.

Tables sort by any column (click its heading; click again to flip the order). Clicking a row opens what it describes: a route, a stop on the map, or a trip's timetable. Long lists show a "Show all" link.

The note under the tabs says when recording started. Numbers with a * are based on fewer than 20 samples, so treat them as rough.

Service

Did the scheduled trips actually run?

For every trip in the timetable: did a bus run all of it, part of it, none of it, or did BCT cancel it?

  1. 1
    The headline. The share of trips that a bus ran from start to end, out of the trips that could be judged.
  2. 2
    Every trip falls in one group:
    • In full: a bus ran the whole trip.
    • Only partly: GPS first saw the bus 15+ minutes into the trip, or last saw it 15+ minutes before the end. A bus whose GPS failed mid-trip looks the same (see the Feed tab).
    • Canceled: BCT's feed marked it canceled.
    • Missed without notice: no bus ever showed up, and BCT never said it was canceled.
    • Not judged: the recorder wasn't running for the whole trip, so it can't say.
  3. 3
    By day: one bar per day, split into those groups, with the "in full" share on the right. It follows the route picker, and in the Today view it's a single bar for today. Days before recording began are left off.

A trip is only judged once it has been over for half an hour.

  1. 4
    By route. The same groups for each route, worst first. Click a row to narrow every tab to that route.
  2. 5
    Ran only partly. Each partial trip, and which end wasn't run ("first 18 min not run", "last 22 min not run"). Click one to open its timetable.
  3. 6
    Missed without notice. Trips that never ran and were never announced. These are the ones riders waited for in vain.
  4. 7
    Canceled trips. Every trip BCT marked canceled, with what a bus actually did: what share of the trip's stops a bus served ("so far" while it's still running), whether BCT put it back on the schedule, or that no bus was seen. Click one for the full story.

Clicking a canceled trip opens its details on the map side. Besides the verdict, it shows:

  • when it was due, and how much notice BCT gave before cancelling;
  • the bus that was assigned, and BCT's alert text;
  • the gap riders faced: the trips just before and after it, so you can see how long the wait became.

In the last week of October, GPS saw a bus run about 3 in 4 of the trips BCT marked canceled.

On time

Do buses leave their stops on time?

Measured the way US transit agencies do it: at each timepoint (the main stops with a time printed in the timetable), how early or late the bus left.

  • On time: no more than 1 minute early and no more than 5 minutes late.
  • Early: more than 1 minute early. Worse than it sounds: riders who come on time miss the bus.
  • Late: more than 5 minutes late.

The time comes from GPS: the moment the bus is seen passing the stop, or pulling away from its first stop.

  1. 1
    All: every timed departure in the period, split into early, on time and late. The number on the right is the on-time share.
  2. 2
    By route: the same bar for each route. Click a route to see its hours: the same bars for each hour of the day, which shows when that route struggles (often the afternoon rush).

A route with a big yellow slice runs early too often: its timetable may be too loose, or drivers may be leaving early.

Predictions

Can riders trust the countdown?

BCT's feed predicts when each bus will reach each stop, and keeps updating that guess as the bus moves. This tab checks those guesses against when GPS saw the bus actually arrive.

For each bus and each timepoint ahead of it, the app saves BCT's guess once in each of five windows: 20–30, 10–20, 5–10, 2–5 and 0–2 minutes before the bus is due. When GPS sees the bus pass, each saved guess is graded:

  • Accurate: the bus came no more than 1 minute before the guess, or 3 minutes after.
  • Bus came early: more than 1 minute before the guess. The worst case, because someone trusting the countdown misses the bus.
  • Bus came late: more than 3 minutes after the guess.
  1. 1
    One row per window. "10–20 min: 48%" means that of the guesses made 10 to 20 minutes ahead, 48% were accurate. Guesses made further ahead are naturally worse; the question is by how much.
  2. 2
    By route: overall accuracy, accuracy 5–10 minutes out (when most riders check), how often the bus came early, the average miss, and how many guesses were graded.

Only timepoints are graded, since that's where GPS timing is reliable. The live Worker checks once a minute, so a short window (like 0–2 minutes) is sometimes skipped for a bus.

Headways

On frequent routes, how much longer do riders wait?

On routes with a bus every 20 minutes or less, people don't check the timetable; they just turn up and wait for the next bus. Then what matters is how evenly the buses are spaced.

Most riders arrive during the long gaps, simply because the long gaps take up more of the clock. So uneven buses make the average wait longer, even with the same number of buses.

  • Average wait: how long a rider who turns up at a random moment waits, given the real gaps between buses.
  • Excess wait: that average wait minus the one the timetable promises. +3 min means riders wait 3 minutes longer than they should. This is the standard industry measure for frequent service.

Gaps are measured at timepoints from GPS, between consecutive buses on the same route and direction.

  1. 1
    All routes: each frequent route and direction, worst first, with its excess wait, average wait, and how many gaps were measured. Click one to narrow to it.
  2. 2
    One route: the excess wait in each part of the day (night, morning peak, midday, afternoon peak, evening), then a table of timepoints, so you can see where along the route the buses bunch up.

BCT's frequent service is mainly routes 1, 18 and 19, plus some trips on 22, 72 and 114. Other routes won't appear here.

Transfers

Do riders make their connections?

At stations where 3 or more routes meet (41 of them, from Broward Central Terminal down), this tab checks whether a rider coming in on one route would catch the connecting bus the timetable gives them.

  • The connection: for each bus that arrives at a hub, the next bus on another route that the timetable has leaving 2 to 30 minutes later.
  • Made: that bus really left at least 2 minutes after the first one really arrived (time to walk between bays).
  • Missed: it left too soon. The rider waits for the next one; the extra wait is how much later that one is timetabled.

Both buses have to be seen at the hub by GPS for a connection to count.

  1. 1
    The headline: the share of timetabled connections that were made.
  2. 2
    By hub, worst first, with the average extra wait when a connection is missed. Click a hub to open its pairs.
  3. 3
    One hub's pairs: from which route to which, how often it was made, the usual wait between the two buses when made, and the extra wait when missed. Click a route badge to narrow every tab to it. "← All hubs" goes back.
Schedule

Is each route's timetable realistic?

It compares how long trips really take, timed by GPS, with how long the timetable says, and splits lateness into leaving late and losing time on the road.

Picture a timetable that says a ride takes 30 minutes, and you time it every day with a stopwatch.

  • Usually 40 minutes: the timetable is too tight; the bus is always late.
  • Usually 20 minutes: the timetable is too loose; the bus runs early and waits.
  • Usually 30: it fits.

A late bus has one of two problems, and they need different fixes:

  1. 1
    It left late. The driver started late, or the bus came in late from its previous trip. Fixed at the terminal: more break time between trips, or more buses.
  2. 2
    It lost time on the road. Traffic, red lights, crowds boarding. Fixed on the street, or by giving the timetable more time.

How a trip is measured. Timepoints are the main stops printed in the timetable with a set time; only those are timed.

  1. 1
    First timepoint seen. GPS notes when the bus pulls away. If it's the trip's first timepoint, leaving more than 2 minutes late is a late start.
  2. 2
    Last timepoint seen. From 1 to 2 is the real running time, compared with the timetable's time for the same stretch.
  3. 3
    Gaps are allowed, within limits. The stretch seen must cover at least 80% of the trip's scheduled time or the trip is left out. The arrival at the very last stop is sometimes missed.

A trip is counted once the bus hasn't passed a new stop for 10 minutes.

  1. 1
    Route. Click a row to zoom into it.
  2. 2
    10%+ over: how often trips took more than 10% longer than scheduled. High means too tight.
  3. 3
    vs timetable: how much longer (+) or shorter (−) trips took on average.
  4. 4
    Late starts: the share of trips that left their first timepoint 2+ minutes late.
  5. 5
    Lost en route: minutes lost (or gained, if negative) between first and last timepoint. Orange: 2+ lost; blue: 2+ gained (running early).
  6. 6
    Runs: trips measured. More is more trustworthy.

Reading columns 4 and 5 together tells you which problem a route has:

  • Many late starts, little lost: the start. Not enough break time, or buses arriving late from the previous trip.
  • Few late starts, lots lost: the road. The timetable doesn't allow for real traffic.
  • Both high: both at once.
  1. 1
    Late starts for this route, and the average start time (+1.2 min: trips usually leave about a minute late).
  2. 2
    Lost along the way: the average a trip loses between its first and last timepoint.
  3. 3
    Where delay builds up: one row per timepoint in route order, first at the top. The bar is how late buses are on average when they get there. A yellow bar is early, blue is late by up to 5 minutes, red is more than 5.
  4. 4
    Look for the jump. Buses go from +1.8 to +5.9 minutes late between Tyler Street and Hard Rock: that stretch is where they lose time. Hover a row for the delay added since the one before; click to open the stop.
  1. 1
    Starts: the trip's scheduled start.
  2. 2
    To: where it's headed.
  3. 3
    Left: how late this trip usually leaves its first timepoint. Orange: 2+ minutes.
  4. 4
    vs timetable: how much longer or shorter its running time is than scheduled.
  5. 5
    Runs: times measured. A weekday trip runs up to 5 times in the 7-day view.

Look for patterns: here the 4:35 and 5:10 PM trips run 10 to 11 minutes over while the rest are close, so the rush-hour timetable needs more time. The CSV has the same per trip, in seconds.

Speeds

How fast do buses move, and where does traffic cost time?

Every time GPS sees a bus go from one stop to the next, that stretch gets a time. Thousands of those make a speed map of Broward's bus streets, using the buses as traffic sensors.

  1. 1
    Time of day (all day, night, morning peak, midday, afternoon peak, evening), and Show on map, which colours every measured stretch on the map by speed. Darker red is slower, so problem streets stand out.
  2. 2
    The summary: the average speed between stops (stops along the way included), how many stop-to-stop runs were measured, and the bus-minutes a day lost to congestion.
  3. 3
    Congestion compares each stretch with its own best time of day. If a stretch takes 30 seconds at night and 90 seconds at 5 PM, that extra minute is time lost to congestion, totalled over every bus in a day.
  4. 4
    Slowest stretches: the lowest speeds with at least 3 runs, whatever the reason (traffic, a busy stop, a turn).

Click a stretch to see its first stop on the map, or a route badge to narrow everything to that route. The timetable isn't used as the yardstick here, because BCT's is rounded to the minute and padded around timepoints.

Bunching

When buses arrive in pairs

Bunching is two buses on the same route and direction arriving together, when the timetable has them well apart. Riders then face one long gap followed by two buses at once.

An event is counted when two buses reach the same stop:

  • within 3 minutes of each other,
  • and within a quarter of the gap the timetable plans between them,
  • on trips timetabled at least 10 minutes apart.

Each pair of buses counts once a day. Times come from BCT's trip updates, and only trips with a bus on them count.

  1. 1
    The total for the period and route, and the daily average. With all routes, a bar chart by route sits under it; click a route to narrow to it.
  2. 2
    By hour of day: one column per hour, so you can see when bunching happens (usually the afternoon rush).
  3. 3
    Events: each one, with where and how close. ▶ Replay plays both buses on the map as moving "ghost" buses around the moment they bunched, from their recorded GPS tracks. "Recording" means the replay is still being captured.
Weather

Does rain make buses late?

Each hour is labelled dry, rain, or heavy rain, from Broward's four airport weather stations, and on-time and running times are compared across them.

  • Rain: 0.01 inches or more in the hour, at the wettest station. Heavy: 0.10 inches or more. A trace counts as dry.
  • Each row shows the hours of that weather, how many departures, the on-time bar, and the average lateness.
  1. 1
    "vs dry" on-time: how many percentage points worse (or better) on-time was in rain, compared with dry weather at the same hours of the day. That's fair: a rainy rush hour is compared with a dry rush hour, not a dry midnight.
  2. 2
    "vs dry" running time: how much longer stop-to-stop runs took in rain, compared in the same way.

Florida showers are often a few miles wide and can miss every station, so treat this as a guide.

Trends

Is it getting better or worse?

Every measure, day by day, so you can see changes over time and pick out bad days.

  1. 1
    Week on week (in the 30-day view): the last 7 days against the 7 before for on time, trips ran in full, predictions, transfers, excess wait, bunching and cancellations. Blue is better, orange worse, whichever way "better" points for that measure.
  2. 2
    Worst days: the three days with the lowest on-time, with that day's rain, cancellations and bunching beside them. "(high)" marks a count well above the period's usual, a likely cause.
  3. 3
    By day: every measure in one sortable table. Sort by On time to find the worst days, or by Rain h to see the wet ones.
  4. 4
    By day of the week (once there's more than a week): on-time averaged per weekday, so you can see if Fridays are consistently worse.

With one route picked, On time, Predictions, Bunching and Canceled follow that route. In full, Excess wait and Transfers are only kept for all routes together, so they show "–". The week-on-week comparison needs two weeks of recording.

Shelters

Which busy stops have no shelter?

A planning list: stops with no shelter in Broward County's bus stop survey, busiest first.

  1. 1
    The count of surveyed stops without a shelter, and how many of those are very busy.
  2. 2
    Stop and its city. Click a row to open the stop on the map.
  3. 3
    Buses: how many buses leave the stop on a weekday, from the timetable. BCT publishes no ridership counts, so this stands in for how many people wait there.
  4. 4
    Bench: whether there's at least somewhere to sit. A busy stop with no shelter and no bench is the strongest case.

Stops the survey doesn't cover are left out (the count is shown below the table). Rain and heat aren't used to rank stops, because the weather is much the same county-wide. The CSV includes each stop's coordinates and lighting.

Feed

How good is BCT's live data?

Everything in this app depends on BCT's live feed. This tab measures its problems, and its CSV is meant as a report you could send to BCT.

  1. 1
    Stale reports: GPS positions more than 2 minutes old when BCT published them. The map shows those buses behind where they really are.
  2. 2
    GPS gaps: a bus on a trip that went silent for 5+ minutes.
  3. 3
    Vanished mid-trip: GPS lost a bus with 15+ timetabled minutes of its trip still to go. Either the GPS failed or the bus was pulled; the data can't tell which.
  4. 4
    Timetable-only predictions: BCT's prediction for a bus's next stop was exactly the timetable time, so it carried no live information.
  5. 5
    Canceled but ran anyway: trips BCT marked canceled that GPS saw running. Riders who trusted the cancellation may have skipped a bus that came.

A by-day table (when there's more than one day) and a by-route table follow. Recording gaps (like a restart) are not counted as buses vanishing.

For every tab

Things to keep in mind

New measures start empty

Most numbers fill in as trips finish. Today alone can be thin; the 7- and 30-day views are steadier.

GPS isn't perfect

A bus whose GPS drops out can look like a trip cut short, and a bus's arrival at its final stop is sometimes not caught.

Averages hide the rush hour

A route can be fine at 10 AM and bad at 5 PM. Use the hourly views and the trip tables to see when.

Recording outages

When the recorder isn't running, trips aren't judged rather than counted as missed. The Service tab's "not judged" count shows how much that affected.
